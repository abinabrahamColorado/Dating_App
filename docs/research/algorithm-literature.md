# Meetwell Algorithm Research — Papers to Build From

Oct 22, 2026 · @Abin Abraham

## Bottom line

Published research supports Meetwell's core design, and it gives Codex concrete, codeable algorithms for each stage. None of this needs proprietary code from Tinder or Hinge. All of it is open, peer-reviewed or published work.

Three findings matter most:

- **Limiting choice is backed by market-design research, not just philosophy.** Economists have shown that on matching platforms, restricting how many people each person can contact can raise welfare for one or both sides by reducing congestion. That is the formal case for "3 introductions a week".
- **The best ranking for one person depends on everyone else's.** Cornell and CyberAgent researchers show that recommending each person their individually best match overloads popular people and lowers total matches. They optimise everyone's list jointly. Meetwell's capacity-limited allocator follows the same principle in a simpler form.
- **Learning must be built on logged probabilities from day one.** Microsoft, Yahoo and Spotify research shows that a new model can only be tested fairly on old data if the old system recorded how likely each choice was. Codex already logs this in M3, which is the foundation for every later model.

The build cards at the end turn each paper into a task Codex can implement, with the milestone and the data needed before it is worth building.

## Why finite introductions

The strongest academic support for Meetwell's "few introductions" design comes from economics, not psychology. When contacting someone is cheap, people send too many messages, receivers cannot screen them all, and good matches get lost in the noise. Limiting actions fixes this.

| Paper                                                                                                                                                                                          | What it shows                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                         | What Meetwell takes                                                                                                                  |
| ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| [Halaburda, Piskorski & Yildirim, _Competing by Restricting Choice: The Case of Matching Platforms_, Management Science (2018)](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/mnsc.2017.2797) | A platform that shows fewer options (their example: eHarmony versus Match.com) can coexist with and charge more than an open-search one. With fewer options per person, the people you contact face less competition, so replies are more likely ([Wharton summary](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/when-fewer-choices-are-worth-more/)). | The business case for curated, limited introductions. The mechanism is reduced competition, not reduced information overload.        |
| [Arnosti, Johari & Kanoria, _Managing Congestion in Matching Markets_, MSOM 23(3) (2021)](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10309697)                                                          | Costly screening plus uncertainty about who is still available creates congestion. Capping how many applications each person can send can raise welfare substantially, in some markets for both sides.                                                                                                                                                | Formal support for the per-week introduction cap and the active-connection cap.                                                      |
| [Kanoria & Saban, _Facilitating the Search for Partners on Matching Platforms_, Management Science 67(10) (2021)](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormnsc/v67y2021i10p5990-6029.html)             | Platforms can raise welfare by restricting what agents can do, even when screening is nearly free. In unbalanced markets, letting only the short side initiate helps.                                                                                                                                                                                 | Two-way blind decisions avoid one side flooding the other. If the Manhattan pool is unbalanced, the side asymmetry is worth testing. |

These are theoretical models of stylised markets, not dating experiments. They show the cap can help; the pilot must still measure the right cap size.

## Reciprocal recommendation

A reciprocal recommender predicts two things, not one: will A like B, and will B like A. A whole research field, mostly built on real dating-site data, studies how to combine the two directions.

| Paper                                                                                                                                                                                                                         | Method                                                                                                                                                                                                     | What Meetwell takes                                                                                                                                                            |
| ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| [Pizzato et al., _Reciprocal Recommenders_, University of Sydney (c. 2010)](https://ls13-www.cs.tu-dortmund.de/homepage/ITWP2010/papers/ReciprocalRecommender.pdf)                                                            | Defines the problem. Keep only pairs both sides' recommenders approve, then score as a weighted mix of both directions. For new users, rely mostly on the other side's view, with a small non-zero weight. | The two-sided filter, which M3 already enforces as bilateral eligibility, and the cold-start rule.                                                                             |
| [Akehurst et al., _CCR: A Content-Collaborative Reciprocal Recommender for Online Dating_, IJCAI (2011)](https://www.ijcai.org/Abstract/11/367)                                                                               | Find people with similar profiles, then use who they liked and who liked them to recommend. Works for brand-new users. Reported 69% versus 35% baseline success at top 10 on a large dating dataset.       | A cold-start method for the first V1 model: "people like you were accepted by people like them".                                                                               |
| [Kleinerman, Rosenfeld, Ricci & Kraus, _Optimally Balancing Receiver and Recommended Users' Importance in Reciprocal Recommender Systems_, RecSys (2018)](https://u.cs.biu.ac.il/~krauss/data/articles/recsys18a-sub1173.pdf) | Score = α·(will I like them) + (1−α)·(will they reply), with α tuned per user. Tested live on 398 dating-app users with 3 recommendations a day.                                                           | A real dating field experiment with our cadence. It raised reply rates and surfaced less-popular users, but people clicked less on what it recommended, a trade-off to expect. |
| [Neve & Palomares, _Latent Factor Models and Aggregation Operators for Collaborative Filtering in Reciprocal Recommender Systems_, RecSys (2019)](https://dsrs.blogs.bristol.ac.uk/?p=546)                                    | Matrix factorisation for each direction, then an aggregation operator to combine them.                                                                                                                     | The V2 version of Spotify-style factorisation, adapted to two directions.                                                                                                      |
| [Palomares et al., _Reciprocal Recommender Systems: Analysis of State-of-Art Literature, Challenges and Opportunities_, arXiv 2007.16120 (2020)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.16120)                                            | Survey of the field: how to fuse two one-way preferences into mutual compatibility.                                                                                                                        | The map of the field for Codex before any model work.                                                                                                                          |

Meetwell multiplies the two directions (both must say yes) rather than adding them, which matches the probability chain in Part 15 of our spec. A weighted sum, as in Kleinerman, is a fallback when the two scores are not calibrated probabilities.

## Allocation under capacity

This is the area closest to what Meetwell actually does each week: choose who gets introduced to whom when every person has limited slots. The research says to optimise everyone's introductions together, not one person at a time.

| Paper                                                                                                                                                    | Method                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                  | What Meetwell takes                                                                                                               |
| -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| [Su, Bayoumi & Joachims, _Optimizing Rankings for Recommendation in Matching Markets_, WWW (2022)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.01941)                     | Maximise the expected number of mutual matches across the whole market. Each person's chance of a reply depends on how many others also contacted that person. Solved with a convex relaxation over doubly stochastic matrices (Frank–Wolfe, Sinkhorn). | The principle behind our allocator: popular people must not be recommended to everyone. A V2 candidate if greedy proves too weak. |
| [Tomita, _MODE: Mutual Optimality in Direct Effects of Reciprocal Recommendations in Matching Markets_, RecSys (2026)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.01731) | Iterative and deterministic: given everyone else's lists, give each person the top-K by expected match gain, and repeat until stable. More matches than baselines on real dating data, and faster.                                                      | The newest and most implementable method here. A strong V1 or V2 upgrade path from greedy, with K = 3.                            |
| [Mestre, _Greedy in Approximation Algorithms_, ESA (2006), pp. 528–539](https://dblp1.uni-trier.de/rec/conf/esa/Mestre06.html)                           | Theory of when greedy algorithms are provably within a constant factor of optimal, including matching-type problems.                                                                                                                                    | Why M3's greedy allocator is a reasonable start; the offline exact-solver benchmark measures the actual gap.                      |
| [De Francisci Morales, Gionis & Sozio, _Social Content Matching in MapReduce_, arXiv 1105.4256 (2011)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1105.4256)                  | Capacity-limited matching at scale (GreedyMR, StackMR) so no one is overwhelmed and every supplier gets a chance.                                                                                                                                       | How to scale the allocator past one city, if that day comes.                                                                      |
| [Singh & Joachims, _Fairness of Exposure in Rankings_, KDD (2018)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.07281)                                                     | Rankings that maximise usefulness while provably meeting a chosen fairness constraint on how exposure is shared.                                                                                                                                        | Formal tools for the exposure audits and concentration limits in Part 16 of our spec.                                             |

Stable matching (Gale–Shapley, used by Hinge) is deliberately not the default. Our spec, Part 16, notes it optimises stability under assumed preferences, not our goal of feasible, mutually wanted meetings under capacity.

## Learning safely from logged data

Every introduction Meetwell makes is also an experiment. These papers show how to learn from those experiments without fooling ourselves, which happens when a model is tested only on the pairs the old system chose to show.

| Paper                                                                                                                                                                     | Method                                                                                                                                                                                                                              | What Meetwell takes                                                                                                                        |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| [Li, Chu, Langford & Wang, _Unbiased Offline Evaluation of Contextual-Bandit-Based News Article Recommendation Algorithms_, WSDM (2011)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1003.5956) | The "replay" method: test a new recommendation policy on logged data from a random policy, with provably unbiased results. Built on Yahoo front-page data.                                                                          | Our 10% random exploration slots are exactly the data replay needs.                                                                        |
| [Dudík, Langford & Li, _Doubly Robust Policy Evaluation and Learning_, ICML (2011)](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/1103.4601)                                                  | Combine a reward model with propensity weighting; accurate if either one is good. Lower variance than weighting alone.                                                                                                              | The estimator to use for the "learning increment" experiment in Part 25 of our spec.                                                       |
| Schnabel et al., _Recommendations as Treatments: Debiasing Learning and Evaluation_, ICML (2016), cited in spec v0.2 as \[M01\]                                           | Treat each recommendation as a treatment; correct for selection bias with propensity weights.                                                                                                                                       | The bias framework behind I-15 exposure logging.                                                                                           |
| [McInerney et al. (Spotify), _Personalizing Explainable Recommendations with Bandits_ (BaRT), US patent 11301513](https://patents.google.com/patent/US11301513)           | Chooses an item and its explanation together with a bandit; logs the probability of each choice; retrains with counterfactual risk minimisation. Explores items and explanations separately to keep propensities from getting tiny. | Spotify's production version of our logging plus exploration design. Also: explanations ("because you…") can be learned, not just written. |
| Johari et al., _Experimental Design in Two-Sided Platforms: An Analysis of Bias_, Management Science (2022), cited in spec v0.2 as \[M02\]                                | A/B tests in marketplaces are biased because treated and control users compete for the same partners.                                                                                                                               | Why matching experiments must be randomised by week, event or cohort, not by member.                                                       |

Schnabel and Johari are carried from the source register in Architecture v0.2; I did not re-open those two papers for this document.

## Borrowed from games and music

These are the cross-domain ideas: take the mechanism, refuse the objective.

**From game matchmaking: uncertainty, not rank.** [Herbrich, Minka & Graepel, _TrueSkill: A Bayesian Skill Rating System_, NeurIPS (2006)](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/trueskilltm-a-bayesian-skill-rating-system/) extends chess Elo by tracking how uncertain each rating is and updating it with Bayesian message passing. Meetwell takes the uncertainty idea: a new member's estimates start wide and narrow only as evidence arrives, so newcomers are never judged on thin data. It refuses the skill ladder itself, which in dating becomes a desirability "league", banned by our spec.

**From game matchmaking: queue widening.** Online games widen the acceptable match range the longer a player waits. I found no peer-reviewed paper on this to cite here; it is common industry practice. M3 already implements a version: waiting time raises priority, and retrieval widens within the member's own boundaries.

**From Spotify: weekly batches, explained picks, and bandits.** Discover Weekly's rhythm is our weekly introduction batch. BaRT (above) shows that the explanation itself ("because you both…") affects whether people engage, and can be learned. Collaborative filtering ("people like you liked…") arrives for Meetwell in its two-sided form via Neve & Palomares, only at V2 scale and only after bias audits.

**From Pokémon GO: public places and time-boxed gatherings.** Its raids gather strangers at a public spot for a set time. This is a product pattern rather than a research result, so there is no paper to cite. It maps onto M6 events: 4 to 8 people, a set time, an approved venue, opt-in only. Map indexing such as Uber's H3 or Google's S2 cells is relevant only for neighbourhood Go, as Part 9 of our spec notes.

## What the research warns against

The same literature sets limits on what any algorithm can promise.

- **Chemistry is hard to predict before people meet.** [Joel, Eastwick & Finkel, _Is Romantic Desire Predictable?_, Psychological Science (2017)](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0956797617714580) used machine learning on more than 100 self-reported traits and could predict who is generally liked, but not which specific pairs would click. Meetwell's algorithm should therefore focus on eligibility, mutual willingness and getting people to meet, not on claiming to predict chemistry.
- **Attention concentrates into hierarchies.** [Bruch & Newman, _Aspirational Pursuit of Mates in Online Dating Markets_, Science Advances (2018)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6082652/) measured a steep, consistent desirability hierarchy using a PageRank-like method on messaging data. That is the Google-style ranking Meetwell must not reproduce.
- **More options can make people more rejecting.** [Pronk & Denissen, _A Rejection Mind-Set: Choice Overload in Online Dating_ (2020)](https://research.tilburguniversity.edu/en/publications/a-rejection-mind-set-choice-overload-in-online-dating/) found acceptance fell about 27% from the first to the last profile in a session.
- **Optimising reply rates has a cost.** In Kleinerman et al. (2018), the method that raised replies also lowered how attractive recommendations looked to the receiver. Expect any reciprocal model to trade one side's appeal against the other's response.
- **Collaborative filtering learns society's biases.** "People like you liked them" reproduces existing preference patterns, including racial ones. Part 14 of our spec requires bias audits before any such model ships.
